DAIRY EQUIPMENT ATLAS • BUTTER PROCESSING

Butter Churner

A batch-processing machine that converts prepared cream into butter through controlled mechanical agitation, fat aggregation, buttermilk separation and subsequent working.

Batch ProductionCream ChurningButter FormationButtermilk SeparationWorking
01 • BATCH CHURNER

Butter churn for batch production

Batch churning is carried out after cream temperature treatment and, where applicable, ripening. The cream is charged into a rotating churn and subjected to repeated agitation until butter granules form. The machine is then used for drainage, optional washing and working of the butter.

Batch butter churn showing the control panel, emergency stop and internal angled baffles.
02 • NUMBERED COMPONENTS

Three highlighted components

These components directly support operation, safety and the mechanical agitation required for butter formation.

1

Control panel

The operator interface for starting and stopping the churn, selecting the appropriate operating speed and observing process conditions. On a modern powered churn, controls may include motor operation, speed selection, timer functions, interlocks and indicators for safe operation.

2

Emergency stop

A dedicated safety control used to rapidly stop the drive when an unsafe condition occurs. It is separate from normal stopping and should remain readily accessible to the operator.

3

Angled baffles

Internal angled surfaces that change the path of the cream as the drum rotates. They increase lifting, falling and rolling of the cream, improving turbulence and repeated exposure of fat globules to mechanical shear.

03 • MACHINE CONSTRUCTION

Major construction features

Churn body

The vessel is commonly cylindrical, conical, cubical or double-conical. Industrial batch churns are generally constructed from hygienic stainless steel and designed for controlled rotation.

Drive and transmission

An electric motor and transmission rotate the churn at selectable speeds. Different speeds are useful because the intense agitation required for churning is different from the gentler movement used during washing and working.

Access door

A large hygienic door provides loading and unloading access. The closure must remain secure during rotation and should be interlocked where the machine design provides an automated safety system.

Internal working geometry

Angled baffles, strips or other internal surfaces alter the cream trajectory. Modern geometric churns may also obtain sufficient agitation from their shape, edges and surface profile.

Jacket / cooling arrangement

Depending on design, temperature can be controlled before or during processing using chilled water or another cooling medium around the product zone.

Outlet and drainage

Drainage arrangements allow buttermilk and wash water to be removed without opening the entire machine, supporting controlled batch operation and hygienic handling.

04 • EVOLUTION OF CHURNERS

From traditional agitation to modern batch machines

Butter churners have developed around one fundamental requirement: produce enough relative movement between cream and the churn to destabilise the fat emulsion without simply carrying the cream around with the vessel.

Early swinging churns

A suspended vessel was moved back and forth so that cream repeatedly travelled across the vessel. The movement generated the impacts and turbulence needed for butter formation.

Dash churns

The vessel remained stationary while a plunger or dasher moved through the cream. Rotating agitators later provided a more controlled form of mechanical agitation.

Rotating churns

The entire barrel rotates around its axis, allowing the cream to be lifted and dropped repeatedly. Internal geometry can intensify the agitation and influence churning time.

Modern batch designs

Common geometric forms include cylindrical, conical, cubical and double-cone arrangements. Large industrial machines use controlled speed and hygienic stainless-steel construction.

05 • HOW BUTTER FORMS

From an oil-in-water emulsion to butter

Cream is an oil-in-water emulsion: milk-fat globules are dispersed through the aqueous phase. Churning changes this structure into butter, where the continuous phase is predominantly fat and the remaining water is finely dispersed.

06 • FAT GLOBULE MECHANISM

What actually happens inside the cream?

1. Fat crystallisation matters first

Milk fat contains triglycerides with different melting characteristics. Cream temperature treatment is used to create an appropriate crystalline structure before churning. The proportion of solid and liquid fat strongly influences the consistency of the butter that develops.

Harder fat requires a different temperature programme from softer fat. This is why churning should not be treated as a single universal temperature operation.

2. Agitation destabilises the globules

As the churn rotates, the cream is lifted and released. Repeated deformation, collisions and incorporation of air expose the fat globules to strong mechanical action. The protective membrane surrounding the globules is progressively disrupted.

Once the membrane is sufficiently destabilised, released liquid fat can act as a binding phase between neighbouring fat particles.

3. Butter grains appear

Small clusters grow into visible butter granules. The aqueous phase surrounding them becomes buttermilk. At the breaking point, the product changes visibly from a smooth cream to distinct butter grains suspended in liquid.

4. Phase inversion is completed by working

After drainage, the butter grains are worked together. The fat becomes a continuous matrix and the remaining water is subdivided into very small droplets. Properly worked butter should not show visible droplets of water.

The key transformation

Cream = oil-in-water emulsion → churning destabilises fat globules → butter grains form → working produces a continuous fat phase containing finely dispersed water.

07 • AGITATION PHYSICS

Why churn speed is critical

The objective is not simply to rotate the drum as fast as possible. The cream must repeatedly fall, roll and collide so that useful turbulence is created.

Too little speed

If the rotational speed is too low, the cream receives insufficient mechanical agitation. Fat-globule collisions are less frequent and churning may take longer, with poorer separation performance.

Too much speed

If rotational speed becomes excessive, centrifugal action can press the cream against the outer wall so that it travels with the drum rather than falling through it. The useful agitation can then decrease instead of increasing.

Useful churning condition: ω²R < g   →   n < (1 / 2π) √(g / R)

Here, ω is angular velocity, R is the effective radius and g is gravitational acceleration. This relationship is a simplified physical limit for avoiding complete wall-pinning; actual industrial speed selection also depends on churn geometry, fill level, cream properties and internal fittings.

08 • LOADING

Why the churn is not filled completely

40–50%Typical industrial batch-churn fill range, leaving space for movement and foaming.
1/3A common working load for some smaller or practical batch-churn procedures.
8,000–14,000 L+Large industrial batch-churn capacities can reach this range.
15–30 rpmA common industrial-speed range for large batch churns; machine-specific settings vary.

Overloading

Too much cream reduces the free space needed for the cream to be lifted and dropped. The intensity of the movement falls, foaming space is reduced and churning can become inefficient.

Underloading

Very low fill reduces the amount of cream participating in useful impacts and makes the equipment less economical to operate. The cream may also tend to coat the walls rather than move through the intended trajectory.

09 • TEMPERATURE CONTROL

Churning temperature and fat consistency

Temperature treatment before churning determines the physical condition of milk fat. Churning temperature is therefore selected according to the fat composition and desired butter consistency.

Typical churning rangeAbout 7–13 °CCommon practical range; the exact temperature is selected according to fat characteristics and season.
Hard-fat treatmentCool to ~8 °C → hold → gently heat to 20–21 °C → cool to ~16 °C → churning temperatureReduces mixed-crystal formation and increases the liquid-to-solid fat ratio to improve consistency.

10 • OPERATING SPEEDS

Churning, washing and working are not the same operation

Churning speed

The highest useful speed is used to create strong agitation and rapid butter-grain formation. Large industrial churns commonly operate in the approximate 15–30 rpm range, while smaller machines can use substantially different speeds.

Washing speed

After buttermilk drainage, slower rotation is used when chilled wash water is introduced. The objective is gentle contact and washing rather than maximum agitation.

Working speed

Still slower rotation can gather the grains and distribute moisture. Excessive working can alter texture and can cause an undesirable greasy or overly soft body depending on fat condition.

11 • BATCH PROCESS

Complete operating sequence

01 • Prepare
Inspect the cream for sensory quality and suitability. Ensure the required fat composition and temperature treatment have been achieved.
02 • Load
Charge the churn to the selected working level, commonly around 40–50% of capacity in industrial batch churns, and secure the door.
03 • Churn
Rotate at the selected churning speed. The cream is repeatedly lifted and dropped, producing turbulence and mechanical disruption of the fat globule membranes.
04 • Break
Continue until butter grains are clearly formed and the buttermilk becomes visibly more watery. Avoid excessive churning after the desired grain condition is reached.
05 • Drain
Stop or reduce operation as required by the machine and drain the buttermilk. The drained liquid can be checked for fat losses as part of process control.
06 • Wash
Where washing is used, add chilled water in the required quantity and rotate at a lower speed. Drain the wash water after the desired contact time.
07 • Work
Rotate at low speed to consolidate the butter grains and distribute moisture. Salt, when used, is incorporated during the appropriate working stage.
08 • Check
Assess moisture, texture, appearance and composition. Continue or adjust working only as necessary to reach the product specification.
09 • Unload
Discharge the finished butter into clean food-contact containers or transfer equipment and move it promptly to packaging and cold storage.
12 • BUTTERMILK SEPARATION

What happens at the breaking point?

Butter grain formation

As globules aggregate, the cream no longer behaves as a uniform liquid. Distinct butter granules become visible and the surrounding liquid becomes buttermilk.

Fat loss control

Good churning conditions produce adequate grain formation and effective separation. Excessive fat in the drained buttermilk indicates avoidable process losses and can be used as a diagnostic indicator.

13 • WORKING

How butter texture is developed after churning

Gathering

Individual butter grains are compressed and gathered into a coherent mass.

Moisture dispersion

The water phase is divided into fine droplets and distributed through the continuous fat phase.

Body and spreadability

The final structure depends on fat crystallisation, working intensity, temperature and moisture distribution.

Visual quality check

Properly worked butter should appear coherent and dry rather than showing visible free water droplets. Moisture should be checked regularly because composition and texture are closely linked.

14 • HYGIENE & CLEANING

Sanitary care of a batch churn

Because the churn directly contacts cream and butter, cleaning must prevent residual fat, milk solids and microorganisms from becoming a source of contamination or off-flavour.

Routine hot-water cleaning

An example batch-churn cleaning regime uses hot water at approximately 80–90 °C, with the churn containing about 10–12% of its capacity. A fat-emulsifying alkaline cleaner may be used at around 0.2–0.3%, followed by rotation for several minutes, draining and thorough rinsing.

Mineral-deposit removal

For persistent mineral deposits, an alkaline cleaning stage may be followed, after a thorough intermediate rinse, by an acid descaling stage. A practical example is 2% caustic solution at ~60 °C for 15 min, followed by rinsing and 0.5% nitric acid at ~60 °C for 15 min, then a final rinse.

Important operating rule

Never mix alkaline and acid cleaning chemicals. Drain and rinse completely between stages, and use only chemical concentrations, temperatures and contact times approved for the actual churn, seals, elastomers and plant cleaning programme.

18 • ENGINEERING SUMMARY

Butter churner in one picture

Mechanical objective

Create controlled turbulence without wall-pinning. The cream must repeatedly move through the vessel so fat globules collide and aggregate.

Product objective

Convert cream into a coherent butter phase with finely dispersed moisture. Temperature, fat crystallisation, churning, separation and working must act together.
