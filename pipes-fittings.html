01 • OVERVIEW

Sanitary Dairy Piping

A dairy piping system provides the controlled path for milk and milk products between tanks, pumps and process equipment. It must provide reliable flow while remaining hygienic, cleanable and drainable. A well-planned layout also keeps valves, instruments and maintenance points accessible.

Product lines

Carry milk and milk products between process stages.

CIP lines

Provide controlled circulation of cleaning and sanitising solutions.

Utility lines

Carry services such as water, steam, compressed air and refrigeration media.

02 • MATERIALS

Pipe Materials

Product-contact piping is generally made from corrosion-resistant stainless steel with a smooth internal surface. AISI 304 is common for general dairy service, while AISI 316/316L is selected when greater corrosion resistance is required.

AISI 304

Widely used for sanitary product piping, fittings, tanks and general plant construction.

AISI 316 / 316L

Useful where improved resistance to aggressive cleaning conditions or chloride-containing environments is required.

Food-grade polymer tubing

Used selectively for flexible or specialised services where temperature, pressure and chemical compatibility are suitable.

03 • HYGIENIC DESIGN

Key Hygienic Design Principles

Product-contact surfaces should be smooth, accessible for cleaning and arranged to minimise product retention. The design should avoid unnecessary crevices, stagnant branches, exposed threads and difficult-to-clean pockets.

Smooth surfaces

Reduce retention and support effective cleaning.

Drainable routing

Lines should be arranged so residual liquid can move toward designated drain points.

Minimal dead legs

Avoid unnecessary blind branches and stagnant zones.

Hygienic joints

Permanent welds should be smooth and properly finished; detachable joints should be suitable for hygienic service.

Accessible equipment

Valves, instruments and service points should remain accessible for inspection and maintenance.

Suitable supports

Supports should prevent sagging, vibration and unwanted movement without creating soil traps.

04 • PIPE SIZING

Pipe Diameter and Flow

Pipe diameter is selected from the required flow rate, acceptable velocity, pressure loss, product properties, cleaning requirements and economic considerations. Oversizing increases installation cost; undersizing can increase velocity and pressure loss.

Basic flow relationship

Q = A × v     and     A = πD² / 4

For a circular pipe, Q is volumetric flow rate, A is internal flow area, v is average velocity and D is internal diameter.

Practical point: Use the actual internal diameter of the selected sanitary tube when calculating velocity. Nominal size and outside diameter are not automatically the same as the internal flow diameter.

Pump suction

The suction side should be designed to avoid excessive velocity and unnecessary pressure loss. The suction line is commonly kept at least as large as the discharge line and may be larger where required by the pump and process conditions.

05 • SLOPE & DRAINAGE

Pipe Slope and Complete Drainability

Pipe slope is provided to allow liquid remaining in the line to move by gravity toward a designated drain point. The important principle is not that every pipe in a plant must be sloped, but that lines which require gravity drainage are routed with a continuous fall and without unintended low pockets.

Continuous fall

Maintain a consistent downward route toward the selected drain point where drainability is required.

Low-point drain

Place the drain at a genuine low point so residual liquid has a path to leave the line.

Avoid pockets

An isolated low section between higher points can retain product or cleaning liquid even when the rest of the line appears sloped.

Typical design value: A slope of about 0.3–0.5% is commonly used where complete drainage by gravity is required. Final slope should follow the actual process, piping standard, installation tolerances and equipment arrangement.

Important distinction: A holding tube or other process line may have a specific hydraulic and process requirement, while a drainable product/CIP line is arranged primarily to prevent retained liquid. Therefore, slope should be decided line-by-line rather than applying one rule blindly to every pipe in the plant.

07 • CONNECTIONS

Pipe Connections

Common sanitary arrangements include orbital or manual hygienic welding and detachable sanitary clamp or union connections. The selection depends on whether the joint must be permanently closed or frequently opened for inspection, maintenance or equipment removal.

Welded joint

Creates a permanent connection with few detachable interfaces. Proper alignment, welding and internal finishing are essential.

Clamp connection

Allows rapid dismantling while maintaining a hygienic connection when the gasket and ferrules are correctly fitted.

Union

Provides a serviceable connection between pipe sections or equipment using a sanitary joint arrangement.

08 • VALVES

Sanitary Valves

Valves may isolate a section, regulate flow, divert product or safely separate different media. Selection must consider product characteristics, pressure, temperature, cleaning method and required hygienic performance.

Butterfly valve

Compact quarter-turn valve commonly used for isolation in hygienic lines.

Diaphragm valve

Uses a flexible diaphragm to control or isolate flow while keeping the operating mechanism separated from the process medium.

Mixproof valve

Allows different process media to be routed through a valve matrix while reducing the risk of cross-contamination between separated circuits.

09 • SUPPORTS

Pipe Supports and Layout

Supports keep the piping at its intended elevation and slope while limiting vibration and excessive movement. They should be placed at suitable intervals and near heavy components, valves and changes in direction. Hygienic installation also requires the support arrangement to avoid areas where dirt or moisture can accumulate.

  • Maintain the designed pipe slope after installation.
  • Provide adequate support near valves, fittings and equipment connections.
  • Allow for thermal expansion and contraction where temperature changes are significant.
  • Use suitable corrosion-resistant support materials in hygienic areas.
  • Avoid rigid arrangements that can transfer excessive thermal or mechanical loads to equipment nozzles.
10 • INSTRUMENTATION

Instruments on Process Lines

Temperature, pressure, flow and sampling devices are installed at locations where the measurement represents the actual process condition and where the connection can still be cleaned and maintained.

Pressure gauge

Indicates line pressure and helps operators identify abnormal pressure conditions.

Temperature sensor

Measures product or utility temperature at a representative point.

Flow meter

Measures volumetric or mass flow according to the selected instrument technology.

11 • PIPING DOCUMENTATION

P&ID and Isometric Drawings

A P&ID communicates the process relationship between equipment, piping, valves and instruments. A piping isometric provides the three-dimensional routing information needed to fabricate or install the line. Standard symbols and consistent line identification make the system easier to operate, inspect and maintain.

Remember: A P&ID explains what is connected and controlled; an isometric drawing explains how the piping is physically routed.
12 • QUICK CHECKLIST

Before Accepting a Dairy Product Line

  • Is the material compatible with the product, cleaning chemicals, temperature and pressure?
  • Are product-contact surfaces smooth and free from avoidable crevices?
  • Are valves and instruments accessible?
  • Is the selected diameter suitable for the required flow and pressure loss?
  • Does the drainable portion of the line have a continuous fall toward its drain point?
  • Are there unintended low pockets or difficult-to-clean branches?
  • Are supports maintaining the intended route and allowing for thermal movement?
  • Are welded and detachable joints properly finished and assembled?
