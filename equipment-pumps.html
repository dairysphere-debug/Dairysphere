Sanitary Pumps

Pumps are the transport system of a dairy plant: they move milk, cream, water, cleaning solutions and other process fluids through tanks, heat exchangers, filters, separators and pipelines. Correct selection depends on flow, product properties and system pressure.

The basic job

The pump converts mechanical energy from the motor into fluid energy. In a centrifugal pump, the impeller raises the liquid's velocity and the casing converts part of that velocity into pressure. In a positive displacement pump, a defined volume is trapped and displaced during each revolution or stroke.

Selection rule

Start with flow rate, product, viscosity, density, temperature and required pressure/head — then choose the pump type and size.

02 • MAIN PUMP FAMILIES

Which pump for which dairy duty?

◉

Centrifugal pump

The most widely used dairy pump. Best suited to relatively low-viscosity liquids that do not require particularly gentle treatment.

Typical duties: milk transfer, water, low-viscosity product circulation and many general process services.

↻

Positive displacement pump

Moves a defined volume per revolution or stroke. Well suited to higher-viscosity products and duties requiring controlled flow.

Examples: lobe-rotor, twin-screw and reciprocating pumps.

◎

Liquid-ring pump

Designed for liquids containing significant amounts of air or gas where a conventional centrifugal pump may lose prime.

Common duty: aerated CIP return streams.

03 • CENTRIFUGAL PUMP

How a centrifugal pump works

Step 1 — suction

Liquid enters the pump through the suction connection and reaches the centre, or eye, of the impeller.

Step 2 — acceleration

Rotating impeller vanes set the liquid in motion and increase its velocity.

Step 3 — pressure conversion

The liquid leaves the impeller at higher velocity and pressure. In the pump casing, part of the velocity is converted into pressure before discharge.

Step 4 — delivery

The discharge line carries the product to the next process step. System resistance determines the head the pump must develop.

04 • POSITIVE DISPLACEMENT

How PD pumps differ

Defined displacement

For each revolution or reciprocating movement, a definite net amount of liquid is moved. At lower viscosities, internal slip can increase as pressure rises.

Pressure protection is essential

Closing a valve downstream of a positive displacement pump can cause pressure to rise dramatically. A pressure-relief device or bypass is therefore required, and the discharge path must not be unintentionally blocked.

05 • PUMP TYPES IN DAIRY PLANTS

Construction and application guide

1

Standard centrifugal

General-purpose pump for most non-viscous dairy liquids.

2

High-inlet-pressure centrifugal

Designed for duties where the pump must withstand higher inlet pressure, such as some filtration systems.

3

Multi-stage centrifugal

Uses several impellers/stages to achieve higher outlet pressure at relatively low capacities; useful as a booster pump.

4

Self-priming centrifugal

Special construction for aerated applications, including CIP return service.

5

Lobe-rotor

Two independently driven lobed rotors create cavities that draw product in and move it around the casing to discharge.

6

Twin-screw

Two contra-rotating screws form volumetric chambers. It combines positive-displacement process duties with strong CIP capability and low pulsation.

7

Diaphragm / dosing

Positive-displacement pumps used when gentle transfer or controlled metering is required; pneumatic versions produce pulsating flow.

8

Liquid-ring

Handles air/gas-containing liquids and is useful where a standard centrifugal pump would lose prime.

07 • SUCTION & NPSH

Why the suction line matters

Keep the suction side favourable

The pump should be installed as close as practical to the source tank, with a short and well-designed suction line and as few unnecessary bends and valves as possible.

High pressure losses on the suction side can reduce the available pressure at the pump inlet and increase the risk of cavitation.

NPSH in simple terms

NPSH represents the pressure margin available at the pump inlet above the liquid's vapour-pressure requirement. The available NPSH must be sufficient for the selected pump at the required flow.

Safe operation → NPSH available > NPSH required
08 • FLOW CONTROL

How pump capacity can be adjusted

Throttling

Restricting the discharge changes the operating point. It is flexible, but can be less energy-efficient.

Impeller trimming

Reducing impeller diameter can provide a more economical fixed adjustment for centrifugal pumps.

Speed control

Changing pump speed is flexible and can be energy-efficient, especially when variable operating conditions are expected.

09 • DAIRY APPLICATIONS

Where pumps appear in the plant

Milk reception & storage

Transfer of raw milk from collection/reception systems to storage and processing tanks.

Pasteurization

Feed and booster pumps maintain controlled product flow and pressure through the heat-treatment system.

CIP

Cleaning solutions are circulated through pipelines and equipment. Pump selection must account for chemical service, temperature and possible air entrainment.

Membrane filtration

Centrifugal pumps commonly provide feed and circulation; positive displacement pumps can be used for higher-viscosity, high-pressure duties.

Cream & viscous products

Positive displacement pumps are preferred when viscosity and gentle handling make centrifugal pumping unsuitable.

Powder plant feed

High-pressure pumps can feed concentrate to atomisation systems in spray drying.

Operator's mental model

Centrifugal pump = continuous velocity-based pumping for mostly low-viscosity liquids.   Positive displacement pump = controlled volumetric displacement for viscous or specialised duties.   Liquid-ring = aerated service.
