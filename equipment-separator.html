EQUIPMENT ATLAS • CENTRIFUGAL SEPARATION

Cream Separator

How does a separator take whole milk and split it into cream and skim milk in a few seconds? The answer lies in centrifugal force, density difference, viscosity and the disc stack.

THE BIG IDEA

Milk → Cream + Skim Milk

A cream separator is a high-speed centrifugal machine used to separate the fat-rich cream phase from the lower-fat skim-milk phase. Milk enters a rapidly rotating bowl, where the density difference between fat globules and skim milk causes the two phases to migrate in opposite directions.

Whole milk→ Feed & distribution→ Rotating bowl→ Cream+ Skim milk
Remember: fat globules are less dense than the surrounding skim-milk phase. In a rotating bowl, they move toward the axis, while the denser skim milk moves toward the periphery.

Quick Process Reference

40–45°C Typical warm separation range

Lower viscosity helps fat globules move through the separation channels.

5,500–6,000 rpm Typical bowl-speed range

A high rotational speed produces the strong centrifugal field needed for separation.

0.04–0.07% Typical residual skim fat

Residual fat is an important practical indicator of separator performance.

Disc stack Core separation zone

Many narrow channels shorten the migration distance of fat globules.

Temperature is not simply “higher is better”. Warming milk lowers viscosity and helps separation, but excessive heating increases thermal load. The validated operating temperature of the particular separator should be followed.
01 • FOUNDATION

What Is a Cream Separator?

A cream separator is a mechanical centrifugal separator designed to divide milk into two liquid streams: a cream phase containing most of the milk fat and a skim-milk phase containing most of the water-soluble and non-fat components.

The separation is possible because milk fat globules have a lower density than the continuous skim-milk phase. Under a strong centrifugal field, the density difference produces different radial movement. The separator does not “filter” fat through a mesh; it separates phases by controlled motion inside a rapidly rotating bowl.

A

Whole milk enters

Milk is fed continuously into the separator and distributed into the rotating bowl.

B

Phases migrate

Fat globules move inward while the denser skim-milk phase moves outward.

C

Two streams leave

Separate outlets recover cream and skim milk at controlled flow conditions.

02 • INSIDE THE BOWL

Working Principle — Detailed

The most important idea is to follow one fat globule as the milk passes through the separator. Its movement is controlled by density difference, globule size, milk viscosity and the centrifugal acceleration.

1

Feed and acceleration

Milk enters the bowl through the feed system and is accelerated until it rotates with the bowl. The incoming product must be distributed smoothly to avoid excessive air entrainment and unstable flow.

2

Creation of the centrifugal field

A rotating element produces radial acceleration. At radius R and angular velocity ω, the ideal centrifugal acceleration is ac = ω²R. This acceleration can be many times larger than gravitational acceleration.

3

Entry into the disc stack

Instead of allowing milk to travel through one large chamber, the bowl uses many closely spaced conical discs. The milk is divided into thin channels, greatly reducing the distance a fat globule must travel to reach the cream side.

4

Fat globules move toward the axis

Because milk-fat globules are less dense than the surrounding liquid, the effective centrifugal migration of the globules is opposite to the outward movement of the denser continuous phase. The globules move toward the rotational axis and combine into the cream phase.

5

Skim milk moves toward the periphery

The denser continuous phase migrates outward toward the bowl wall. Dense impurities also tend to collect in the outer sediment region.

6

Separate outlets recover the phases

Once the two phases are established, the cream-rich inner phase and skim-milk-rich outer phase are directed to their respective outlets. Paring-disc and hermetic/centripetal outlet arrangements are common approaches.

04 • CONSTRUCTION

Components of a Cream Separator

Every component has a specific job. Understanding the bowl, disc stack, feed system, outlets and drive makes the complete machine much easier to understand.

1. Separator bowl

The bowl is the rotating chamber in which centrifugal separation occurs. It must withstand high rotational speed and maintain accurate geometry for stable flow.

2. Bowl hood and lock ring

The hood closes the bowl and forms part of the internal flow path. The lock ring secures the bowl assembly and is a critical safety component.

3. Distributor

The distributor receives the incoming milk and spreads it into the separation system. Proper distribution helps prevent uneven loading of the disc channels.

4. Disc stack

A set of closely spaced conical discs forms many thin channels. This is the principal separation zone and provides a large effective separation area.

5. Distribution holes

Aligned holes in the discs guide the milk into the disc channels. Uniform feeding is important for stable separation.

6. Sediment space

Dense non-liquid material and other impurities tend to move toward the bowl periphery and collect in the sediment region.

7. Spindle

The spindle carries the bowl and transfers rotational motion. Accurate alignment and bearing condition are essential because the bowl operates at high speed.

8. Drive system

The motor and mechanical drive provide the power required to accelerate and maintain the bowl at its operating speed.

9. Cream outlet

The inner, fat-rich phase is collected and discharged through the cream outlet. Outlet pressure and flow influence the final cream composition.

10. Skim-milk outlet

The outer, denser phase is removed through the skim-milk outlet. Residual skim fat is a useful practical indicator of separation performance.

11. Paring disc / centripetal pump

These systems recover rotating liquid and convert part of its kinetic energy into useful outlet pressure.

12. Operating-water system

In self-cleaning designs, operating water can control bowl opening and closing for controlled discharge of accumulated solids.

06 • OPERATING CONDITIONS

Why Warm Milk Separates More Easily

1

Viscosity decreases

As temperature increases within the practical warm-separation range, milk viscosity decreases. Lower viscosity reduces resistance to movement of fat globules.

2

Migration becomes faster

The movement predicted by Stokes-type settling behaviour is inversely proportional to viscosity. Lower viscosity therefore supports faster separation.

3

Process remains controlled

The objective is not maximum temperature. The separator should operate at a validated temperature that balances viscosity, product quality and equipment limitations.

Important: temperature, flow rate, bowl speed, air entrainment, disc condition, fat-globule size and outlet adjustment all influence actual separation.
07 • SEPARATION SCIENCE

Stokes’ Law and Fat-Globule Separation

Stokes’ law describes the terminal velocity of a small spherical particle moving through a viscous fluid when the flow around the particle is in the low-Reynolds-number regime. For milk separation, it helps explain why larger fat globules, a greater density difference and lower viscosity favour faster migration.

v = 2 r² (ρp − ρf) g / 9 μ
v = terminal migration velocity (m/s)
r = radius of the fat globule (m)
ρp = density of the particle / fat globule (kg/m³)
ρf = density of the surrounding fluid (kg/m³)
g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²)
μ = dynamic viscosity of the surrounding fluid (Pa·s)

What the equation tells us

  • v ∝ r²: doubling globule radius increases the theoretical velocity by four times.
  • v ∝ Δρ: a larger density difference increases migration velocity.
  • v ∝ 1/μ: higher viscosity slows migration.
  • v ∝ g: a stronger acceleration increases migration velocity.

Why this matters in dairy processing

Cream separators exploit the same density-driven migration principle, but replace ordinary gravitational acceleration with a much stronger centrifugal acceleration. This makes separation practical at continuous industrial flow rates.

08 • FROM GRAVITY TO CENTRIFUGAL FORCE

Stokes’ Law Inside a Separator

In a separator, the effective acceleration is approximately the centrifugal acceleration:

ac = ω²R
ω = angular velocity (rad/s)
R = radial distance from the axis (m)
Therefore:    vc = 2 r² Δρ ω²R / 9 μ
where Δρ = |ρf − ρp|
Key insight: increasing bowl speed increases angular velocity, and because the centrifugal acceleration contains ω², the theoretical migration tendency rises strongly with speed. Actual separator performance is more complex because flow, disc geometry, coalescence, turbulence and outlet conditions also matter.
09 • WORKED CALCULATION

Stokes’ Law — Numerical Example

Consider a simplified fat-globule calculation. Assume a fat globule radius of 1.0 μm, a density difference of 100 kg/m³, milk viscosity of 2.0 mPa·s, and gravitational acceleration of 9.81 m/s².

r = 1.0 μm = 1.0 × 10−6 m
Δρ = 100 kg/m³
μ = 2.0 mPa·s = 2.0 × 10−3 Pa·s
g = 9.81 m/s²
v = [2(1.0×10−6)²(100)(9.81)] / [9(2.0×10−3)]
v ≈ 1.09 × 10−7 m/s
≈ 0.109 μm/s under the simplified gravitational Stokes-law assumptions.
Now notice the engineering problem: gravitational movement is very slow for a small fat globule. A separator solves this by creating a centrifugal acceleration that is far greater than g.
10 • APPLYING THE SAME IDEA

Worked Centrifugal Example

Suppose a separator bowl operates at 6,000 rpm and consider a radial position of 0.10 m. The centrifugal acceleration is:

N = 6,000 rpm
ω = 2πN/60 = 2π(6000)/60 ≈ 628.3 rad/s
ac = ω²R
ac = (628.3)²(0.10) ≈ 39,478 m/s²
ac/g ≈ 4,025 g

So, at this illustrative radius and speed, the centrifugal acceleration is thousands of times gravitational acceleration. This is why a cream separator can accomplish rapid continuous separation that would be extremely slow under ordinary gravity.

This is a simplified physics example. The actual acceleration varies with radius, and the real separation process is governed by the geometry and hydrodynamics of the complete disc stack rather than by a single free-particle calculation.
11 • PERFORMANCE CALCULATION

Skimming Efficiency

One of the easiest practical checks of a separator is the amount of fat that remains in the skim milk. The lower the residual skim fat, the better the fat-removal performance, provided the comparison is made under consistent operating conditions.

Simple residual-fat indicator

Fat remaining in skim milk (%) = Fat % in skim milk / Fat % in incoming milk × 100
Simplified skimming efficiency (%) = 100 − residual-fat indicator

Example

Incoming milk contains 4.0% fat and the skim milk contains 0.05% fat.

Residual indicator = (0.05 / 4.0) × 100 = 1.25%
Simplified efficiency = 100 − 1.25 = 98.75%

Important distinction

The above is a simple concentration-based indicator. For a true mass balance, calculate the actual quantity of fat entering and leaving with the respective flow rates.

Mass-balance form

Skimming efficiency = [1 − (QsFs)/(QmFm)] × 100
Qm = incoming milk flow; Fm = incoming milk fat fraction
Qs = skim-milk flow; Fs = skim-milk fat fraction
12 • WHAT CHANGES PERFORMANCE?

Factors Affecting Separator Performance

Temperature

Higher temperature within the practical range lowers viscosity and can improve migration.

Flow rate

Excessive throughput reduces effective residence/separation conditions and can increase residual skim fat.

Bowl speed

Higher rotational speed increases centrifugal acceleration, within the equipment's safe design limit.

Fat-globule size

Stokes-type behaviour shows a strong dependence on globule radius; larger globules migrate faster.

Milk viscosity

Higher viscosity resists migration and therefore reduces theoretical separation velocity.

Disc condition

Dirty, damaged or incorrectly assembled discs can disturb flow and reduce separation performance.

Air entrainment

Excessive air can disturb stable phase separation and affect downstream product handling.

Outlet adjustment

Incorrect outlet conditions can change cream composition and skim-milk fat losses.

13 • MACHINE TYPES

Open, Semi-open and Hermetic Separators

Open separator

The product path has greater exposure to atmospheric conditions. The design is comparatively simple and is suitable for applications where the process configuration permits it.

Semi-open / paring-disc

A paring device recovers rotating liquid and converts part of its kinetic energy into pressure for discharge.

Hermetic separator

Product remains in a closed, filled system. Centripetal/paring arrangements allow the separated phases to leave without an open product-air interface.

14 • CLEAN OPERATION

Solids Removal and Self-cleaning

Centrifugal separation does more than separate fat. Dense foreign material and sediment tend to migrate toward the outer region of the bowl. In self-cleaning designs, accumulated solids can be discharged through a controlled bowl-opening sequence.

Why solids collect at the periphery

Dense particles experience strong outward centrifugal migration and therefore move toward the bowl wall, where the sediment space is provided.

Self-cleaning concept

Hydraulic operating water can control the sliding bowl bottom. A short controlled opening releases accumulated solids before the bowl closes again.

15 • SAFE OPERATION

Maintenance and Safety

Inspect gaskets and O-rings for wear.
Maintain correct lubrication where applicable.
Check spindle and drive bearings.
Keep discs clean and undamaged.
Assemble the bowl and disc stack correctly.
Never work on a rotating separator.
Verify spindle direction after motor maintenance.
Do not overload the separator with piping forces.
Operate at the specified feed pressure.
Follow the equipment's approved operating and cleaning procedure.
Safety first: the separator bowl stores substantial rotational energy. Assembly, disassembly and inspection must only be performed after the equipment has been safely stopped, isolated and made safe according to the plant procedure.
